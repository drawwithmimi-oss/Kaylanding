Two anteaters walk into a med school. How character-led animation helped UC Irvine talk about behavioral health.

UC Irvine School of Medicine needed working clinicians to lean in — not scroll past — when it launched two behavioral-health fellowships: one in geriatric care, one in maternal mental health. These are subjects people look away from. So instead of inventing a new messenger, we recruited one the whole campus already loves — UCI's own mascot, the anteater — and gave him a white coat (and a colleague).

The Challenge

Vital topics. Exhausted audience.

The Train New Trainers fellowships teach primary-care clinicians to become behavioral-health champions in their communities — one track in geriatric behavioral health, one in maternal behavioral health.

The audiences are the busiest humans alive: physicians, NPs, PAs, and pharmacists; midwives, doulas, and community health workers. And the subject matter — late-life depression, perinatal mood disorders, suicide risk — is exactly the kind of content that triggers scroll-past.

Stigma-adjacent subjects don’t need a louder message. They need a friendlier messenger.

The Insight

Don’t invent a stranger. Borrow a friend.

UC Irvine already had something most health campaigns would kill for: a beloved mascot with decades of built-in recognition. Research on spokes-characters shows that trust in a character transfers to the message it carries — so rather than design a stranger from scratch, we adapted the anteater every UCI alum already smiles at into a clinician-narrator, and designed Petra as his brand-new counterpart.

Peter the Anteater

Mascot, Adapted · Geriatric Behavioral Health (PC-iHOPE)

UC Irvine's official mascot, reimagined by our studio as a clinician — white coat, stethoscope, fun sneakers, permanent thumbs-up. Peter walks clinicians through the 5 Ms of geriatric care with optimism and zero condescension.

Character © UC Irvine · Art by Lipp Studios

Petra the Anteater

Character, Created · Maternal Behavioral Health (PC-MBH)

An original counterpart we designed from the mascot's DNA — pearls, rosy cheeks, and a clipboard with a heart. Petra guides audiences through maternal mental health across the reproductive life cycle — warmth with authority.

The Approach

Charm, held to clinical standards.

🎬 Parallel, modular structure

Both films share a five-beat spine — Intro, Problem, Solution, Curriculum, Call to Action — so any section cuts cleanly for social, email, or conference use.

📋 Facts stay facts

Curriculum visuals locked to source documents: the real 5 Ms of geriatrics, the real reproductive life-cycle topics. Charm is never a license to invent.

♿ Accessibility as design

Captioned and contrast-checked to Section 508 / WCAG — down to redrawing a single yellow connector that measured 1.52:1 against white. Nobody would notice. That’s the point.

🎨 Their brand, their world

UCI blue and gold throughout, Montserrat and Hepta Slab, and real faculty likenesses in the curriculum scenes — the films feel unmistakably theirs.

🎙️ One voice, two characters

A custom-designed narrator voice — earnest, gently playful, mid-paced — tuned so Peter and Petra sound like family.

🔁 Built to compound

A talking-head video is spent when it airs. A character is an asset — every future flyer or slide Peter fronts inherits the recognition built by the last one.

The Evidence

Why characters work for mental health messaging

This isn’t just designer intuition — character-led health communication has receipts.

🤝

Familiarity is borrowed trust

Advertising research shows trust in a spokes-character transfers to the message and brand it carries. A mascot with decades of goodwill gives a campaign a head start no new asset can match.

Garretson & Niedrich, Journal of Advertising (2004)

🌡️

Characters lower the temperature

Animated characters make stigmatized health topics more approachable and less intimidating — audiences engage with a relatable proxy where they’d deflect an institution, and characters can model help-seeking itself.

e.g. What’s Up With Everyone? (2022)

🌍

Animation travels

Short animated storytelling is being trialed at scale for stigma reduction precisely because it leaps over barriers of language, culture, and literacy that live-action and text can’t.

Stanford et al., multi-country RCT protocol
